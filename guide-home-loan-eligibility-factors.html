What Decides How Much Home Loan You Get

Updated November 2026

Banks do not pick a home loan amount at random. They check how much of your income can safely go to EMIs, how steady that income is and how well you have handled credit. Knowing the factors helps you prepare before you apply. You can test your own numbers on our home loan eligibility calculator.

Key takeaways

  • Your monthly income and existing EMIs matter most, because they set the EMI the bank thinks you can carry.
  • A longer tenure lowers the EMI and raises the loan you qualify for, but you pay more interest overall.
  • A good credit score, a co-applicant and fewer running loans can all improve the amount offered.

1. Income and FOIR

Lenders mainly look at the fixed obligation to income ratio (FOIR): the share of your net monthly income that already goes to loan EMIs, including the new one. Most banks allow total EMIs of about 40% to 60% of net monthly income, and higher incomes usually get a higher limit. The maximum EMI for a new loan works out as:

Max EMI = (income × FOIR) − existing EMIs

That EMI is then converted into a loan amount using the interest rate and tenure, which is the normal EMI formula run backwards.

2. Existing EMIs

Every rupee already going to a car loan, personal loan or credit card EMI reduces what is left for the home loan. Banks look at your outstanding dues and minimum payments, not the full credit card limit, though large unpaid balances can still lower your offer.

3. Age and tenure

The tenure you choose changes the loan you qualify for, because the same EMI supports a bigger loan over more years. Lenders also check your age and how many working years you have left, so a younger borrower can usually be offered a longer tenure. The exact age limits differ by lender, so check with your bank.

4. Credit score

A CIBIL score above 750 usually gets better rates and fewer questions. A lower score may mean a higher rate, a smaller loan or a refusal.

5. Property value and LTV

The bank lends against the value of the property, not the price you hope to pay. Lenders usually finance up to 75% to 90% of the property value, depending on the loan size. This is called the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio. You pay the rest as a down payment, plus stamp duty and registration. Even if your income supports a bigger loan, the LTV caps what you can borrow for a particular property.

6. Co-applicant

A working spouse or parent can apply with you. Both incomes are added together, and both credit scores are checked. This can raise the loan amount a lot, as the example below shows.

Worked example (illustrative)

Say you earn ₹1,00,000 a month, pay ₹10,000 in other EMIs, and the bank allows 50% of income for EMIs. The most you can pay as a new EMI is ₹50,000 − ₹10,000 = ₹40,000. At 8.5% for 20 years, that EMI supports a loan of about ₹46.1 lakh. If the bank lends 80% of the value, the property budget is about ₹57.6 lakh.

  • Tenure: the same ₹40,000 EMI at 8.5% supports about ₹32.3 lakh over 10 years and about ₹52 lakh over 30 years.
  • FOIR: at 40% the EMI room is ₹30,000 and the loan is about ₹34.6 lakh. At 60% it is ₹50,000 and the loan is about ₹57.6 lakh.
  • Co-applicant: adding a co-applicant who earns ₹50,000 a month raises total income to ₹1,50,000, the EMI room to ₹65,000 and the loan to about ₹74.9 lakh.

These figures are estimates. Your bank will also check job stability, property papers and documents, so the actual offer can differ.

Ways to improve your eligibility

  • Add a co-applicant with a steady income.
  • Clear or reduce small loans and card balances first.
  • Keep your credit score healthy.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good FOIR for a home loan?

Most banks are comfortable when total EMIs are within 40% to 50% of net monthly income. Some go to 60% for higher incomes or strong credit profiles. Keeping it lower leaves room for savings and emergencies.

Does my spouse’s income count?

Yes, if they apply as a co-applicant. Both incomes are added together, and both credit scores are checked.

Does the bank lend the full price of the house?

No. Lenders usually finance up to 75% to 90% of the property value, depending on the loan size. You pay the rest as a down payment, plus stamp duty and registration.

Is the calculator result the loan I will get?

No. It is an estimate based on income and EMIs. Your bank also checks your credit score, job stability, age, property papers and documents.
