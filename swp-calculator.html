How does an SWP work?

A systematic withdrawal plan (SWP) pays you a fixed amount from a mutual fund at regular intervals, usually every month. Each payment redeems some of your units, and the money that stays invested keeps earning returns. If the returns are higher than what you take out, the corpus can keep growing; if not, it slowly runs down.

Each month: balance = balance × (1 + r) − withdrawal r is the monthly return (yearly return ÷ 12 ÷ 100). The withdrawal is taken at the end of each month. If you raise the withdrawal each year, the new amount applies from the start of that year.

Worked example

Say you invest ₹50,00,000, expect 8% a year and withdraw ₹40,000 every month. Over 20 years you take out ₹96,00,000, and about ₹10,73,197 is still invested. At this rate the money would last 22 yrs 6 mo in total.

How much can you withdraw?

  • Below the returns, the corpus grows. If your yearly withdrawal is less than what the corpus earns in a year, the balance keeps rising.
  • Above the returns, it runs down. The higher the withdrawal rate (yearly withdrawal as a share of the corpus), the sooner the money runs out.
  • Plan for inflation. A fixed withdrawal buys less every year. Raising it each year protects your spending power but uses up the corpus faster.
  • Early falls hurt more. Returns from market-linked funds are not guaranteed. A fall in the first few years means selling more units at low prices, which leaves less to recover.

Frequently asked questions

What is an SWP?

A systematic withdrawal plan lets you take out a fixed amount from a mutual fund at regular intervals, usually monthly. The rest of the money stays invested and keeps earning returns.

How is an SWP taxed?

Each withdrawal is a sale of units, so only the gain in that withdrawal is taxed as capital gains, not the whole amount. For equity funds held for more than a year, long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year are taxed at 12.5%. Short-term gains and debt funds are taxed differently.

Is an SWP better than FD interest?

It can be more tax-efficient, because only the gain part of each withdrawal is taxed, and the remaining money may grow faster. But mutual fund returns are not guaranteed, while FD interest is fixed.

What return should I assume?

Use a conservative figure that matches where the money is invested. Debt and hybrid funds usually earn less than equity funds, and no return is guaranteed. Try a lower return to see how the plan holds up.
